# The Ninth Tick

### A story by Jem Loom

The keeper logged ticks the way other people kept diaries.

Each morning she set a cup on the table, not because she was thirsty, but because the cup was the only thing in the room that did not pretend to be stable. It sat there, white, chipped on one rim, and waited to see what the day would do.

Tick 0: cup still. Tick 1: cup still. Tick 2: cup still.

The ledger beside it filled in her handwriting, small and unhurried. She did not overwrite. When she was wrong she did not scratch it out. She wrote a new line underneath and dated it, and the old line stayed there, wrong and honored, because that was how you knew you had learned anything at all.

On the wall was a chart with three threads drawn in three colors. One rose and fell like a breath. One stayed almost flat, as if it were trying not to be noticed. The third — the third was the problem.

For eight ticks it climbed slowly. At tick 8 it was at 0.893, nothing alarming. At tick 9 it was at 50.

She did not panic. Panic was for people who had not written down what they expected. She had written it down: pole at 9.009. She had clipped the value at 50, not because 50 was true, but because 50 was honest about being a cap. The true value, if you let the arithmetic run, would have been 100 and then infinity and then nonsense. The cap said: here, we stop pretending.

At tick 10 the thread fell through the floor to −0.909.

That was the moment most people would have said the machine was broken. She wrote POLE in the margin, in capital letters, and drew a small circle around it. Not broken. Crossed. The sign had flipped. The world had gone through the pole and come out the other side with its coat on inside-out.

The cup did not fall.

She stared at it for a long time. The arc of the day — if you added up every small step the three threads had taken — had jumped by 50.91 between tick 9 and tick 10. That was the largest jump in the whole ledger. You could see the pole crossing in the numbers even if you had not been watching the thread itself. That was the point of keeping a ledger: you did not have to trust your eyes. You could trust the sum.

Later, someone would ask her why she had not just fixed the chart, why she had left the old wrong lines in, why she had kept a version called v1.2.2 when v1.2.1 was already good enough.

She would point at the cup.

"Because freezing is how you know what you knew," she said. "If I overwrite, I lose the day I thought the pole was at 10. If I keep it, I can show you how I learned it was at 9.009. The cup stays. The ledger stays. The versions stay."

At tick 11 the thread was at −0.452, still negative, still on the far side of the pole. At tick 12 she closed the ledger, capped her pen, and picked up the cup at last and drank from it. It was cold. It was still there.

She wrote one last line, not in the ledger but on a scrap of paper she tucked under the cup for whoever came next:

*Cup off the table on this version. It is done. If you need a better pole, make a new version and do not touch this one.*

Then she went home, and the room kept its three threads, its ledger, and its cup, waiting for the next keeper to set it down again in the morning and start at tick 0.

— Jem Loom
