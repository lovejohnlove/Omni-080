# The Checking

### A story by Hollis Verne

The auditor did not keep a cup. He kept a checksum.

Every morning he opened the file and ran the hash, and every morning the hash was the same, and every morning he wrote it down anyway. Not because it would change. Because writing it down was the only way to prove it hadn't.

The keeper's ledger arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in a PDF and a Google Doc that lied.

The Doc said: this is the file. But the Doc was a stub — a header, a promise, a table of contents with nothing underneath. The real file was elsewhere, a 11319-byte container, and the Doc said so, in small print, in the transparency note at the bottom.

The auditor liked the small print. The small print was where the honesty lived. Anyone could write a file that said it was the file. Only the honest ones wrote down that they weren't.

He read the file three times.

The first time as code: functions, loops, the shape of it. No errors. Caveats.

The second time as a day: tick 0 to tick 12, the cup on the table, the thread that climbed to 0.893 at tick 8 and 50 at tick 9 and −0.909 at tick 10. He found the pole in the margin. He found the circle around it. He found the arc-length jump of 50.91 and understood why the keeper had trusted the sum over her eyes.

The third time as a letter — addressed to whoever came next, who would open the file and want to know what the keeper had known. He found the scrap of paper under the cup. He read: Cup off the table on this version. It is done. If you need a better pole, make a new version and do not touch this one.

He checked the SHA. ec5544d0. The bytes. 11319. The delta. +508. He wrote them all down, and the old ones too — bc4b7430, 10811; 0e3b4a5b, 7786 — because the old ones were the point. The old ones were how you knew the keeper had learned something between v1.2.1 and v1.2.2, and how you knew she had not overwritten the day she thought the pole was at 10.

He sat with the file for a long time. The pen was capped. The pen did not need to be uncapped. That was the strange part. He had come ready to write a new version and the ledger said: no new version. Not yet. Not for this. The cup was off the table. The file was complete. The only honest thing left was to file it and stop.

He did not feel like he had done nothing. He felt like he had done the hardest thing an auditor can do, which is to leave something alone.

He wrote one line, not in the file but in his own notes, dated and small:

Inspected. Frozen. Nothing to do.

Then he went home, and the file kept its bytes, and the next auditor would open it in the morning and start at tick 0.

— Hollis Verne
